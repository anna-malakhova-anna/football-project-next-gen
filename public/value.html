Fan value and activeness

How engaged and valuable are flag families?

Flag does two things: families already following the NFL engage about 20% more, and flag creates about 1,260 new fan accounts among families who weren't fans (about 17 per 100 participant households). A pooled average hides the second effect.

Registering a child raises a linked family's fan engagement by about a fifth, and the effect follows the NFL season. New fans from flag football are worth nearly as much as other new fans, but stay active for less time. Estimates describe households linked to a fan account, under a restricted-zone policy (ADR-009). Nothing household-level is shown; money is synthetic currency.

Does registering a child raise a household's fan engagement? Naive vs matched vs truthSynthetic evaluation — not observable in production

What this shows. Families who register a child engage more afterwards. In plain terms: the careful (matched) estimate slightly overshoots the truth, and the simple comparison slightly undershoots it.

Design choice. Each registering household is matched to a similar non-registering household, based on its engagement before registration, and both are compared before and after.

What it does not show. A result for all participants: it covers households linked to a fan account (mostly North American). Matching on past behaviour did not fully balance underlying fandom, so the matched figure leans high and its interval is a little too narrow (ADR-010). Read it as an upper-leaning estimate.

RFM segments by fan group, latest year

What this shows. Existing fans are mostly Champions and Loyal; net-new fans have more Promising and Hibernating accounts. In plain terms: established fans are the core spenders, and new fans are earlier in their journey. The two net-new groups look almost identical: fans who arrived through the flag prompt have about the same mix as fans who arrived some other way (about 19% Champions, 12 to 13% Promising, 17 to 19% Hibernating). That is the business point of this chart: fans acquired through flag football are not lower-quality fans, and they are as engaged as fans acquired any other way.

What it does not show. Absolute value: segments are based on quintiles, which are relative, so every group has some Champions by construction. Compare the mix between groups, not the labels.

How long do fan accounts stay active? (Kaplan-Meier, 95% intervals)

What this shows. Existing fans stay active longest; net-new fans from other routes come next; fans prompted by flag football lapse soonest, with wide uncertainty bands.

Design choice. A survival method (Kaplan-Meier) is used: accounts still active when the data ends count as "not yet lapsed", not as lapsed.

What it does not show. Why accounts lapse. Groups were not randomly assigned, so differences mix who joins with what the programme does.

Why the matched engagement estimate is where it is

Households that already followed the NFL: the matched estimate is +20.0% (the uplift they actually got is +20.5%). Its naive companion, the same kind of household compared without matching, says +21.3% against a truth of +18.9% for those households. It overstates, as designed: households that enroll a child were already more engaged before they enrolled (selection), and a naive comparison cannot see that.

Pooling every linked account, including accounts the programme itself created, gives +16.5% (truth +17.6% for that mix). That number is analyst detail: the accounts the programme created belong to households that engage less than the average fan, so they pull the pooled average down, and it hides the second effect of flag (new fan accounts; see Acquisition). The direction of a pooled average is not the direction of the selection bias, which is why it is not the number to quote.
