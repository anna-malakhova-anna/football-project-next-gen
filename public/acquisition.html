Fan acquisition

How many families become fans, and how many are new?

Flag does two things: families already following the NFL engage about 20% more, and flag creates about 1,260 new fan accounts among families who weren't fans (about 17 per 100 participant households). A pooled average hides the second effect.

Fans created by flag: about 1,264 fan accounts. Counting every new sign-up and scaling it up for unseen families says 2,019, about 1.6 times too many, because many of those families would have become fans anyway. The first chart is the answer; the rest show where it comes from. Funnel and status describe households linked to a fan account; identity resolution is why the denominator is right (a child in several leagues is one household member, not several).

How many fan accounts did the programme add? Incremental vs naive vs the truthSynthetic evaluation — not observable in production

What this shows. The careful estimate of how many fan accounts exist because of flag football sits close to the red tick (the built-in truth). Counting every new sign-up instead overshoots badly once it is scaled up for families the data cannot see. In plain terms: the careful method is right for the right reason, while the linked-only count looks close but is close by accident, and with a different link rate it could be far off.

Design choice. The estimate uses the staggered rollout (markets launching in different years) as a natural experiment, rather than surveying families.

What it does not show. A precise number. The estimate is only as good as the comparison between launched and not-yet-launched markets, and the range is wide because few markets stand behind each year. The linked-only count looks close to the truth only because two errors partly cancel: it credits every sign-up to the programme but ignores families it cannot see.

The drive chart: from registered household to active fan

What this shows. Of all registered households, only about a third can be matched to a fan account; of those, most can be contacted and most are active. In plain terms: among the families we can see, most are engaged, but we can see only about a third of them. The rest have unknown fan status.

Design choice. Unlinked families sit in their own striped lane, labelled "unknown", not "non-fan". Treating them as non-fans would make the programme look worse than it is.

What it does not show. A strict funnel. Contactable and active are both shares of linked households and they overlap: some active families never opted in. Paying and retained fans are not in the executive view yet.

What fan status do registered households have?

What this shows. Registered households of the latest cohort by fan status. In plain terms: most families are invisible to the fan data, and among the visible ones existing fans dominate, so much of what the programme "reaches" was already a fan.

What it does not show. How many unlinked households really are fans: some have an account the identity matcher could not find. Without identity resolution, a child in several leagues would also inflate this household count. This chart covers the latest cohort, while the drive chart covers the latest month, so do not compare the two totals directly.

The funnel: from fan to active to contactable to paying

What this shows. For linked households of the latest cohort, the share that are active, contactable and monetized, by fan status. Step 3 is a share of active households.

What it does not show. Unlinked households (they have no account to be active or contactable) or causes: existing fans differ from net-new fans before they ever register a child.

Reading note. Dormant lapsed households have no bars for Active and Monetized: they are zero by definition (a household that is still dormant is not active, so it cannot be monetized), not missing data.

Event study: new accounts per 1,000 households, by years since a market launchedSynthetic evaluation — not observable in production

What this shows. The extra new accounts in launched markets compared with markets that had not launched yet, by year since launch, with intervals. The only pre-launch point (two years before launch) sits near zero, which is thin support for the comparison, not a pre-trend test; red ticks are the built-in truth.

What it does not show. Anything about households: this is a market-level comparison, which is why it can say what the programme caused where the naive count cannot. Few markets stand behind some years, so the intervals are wide.

Reading note. Only one pre-launch year (two years before launch) is observed, because every market in this synthetic world launches in year 1 or later and year -1 is the reference year. A flat pre-period therefore rests on a single point.
