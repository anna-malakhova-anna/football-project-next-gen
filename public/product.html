2 · Goals to products

How do you turn a goal into a data product?

Five stages turn a goal into a product that someone owns and watches.

How do you turn a goal into a data product?

  1. 1

    Prework

    Who is the customer, what is the value, who consumes it and how, what type of product is it, who owns it, what does it depend on. Nothing is built before this is answered.

    docs/product/discovery.md products/DP4/canvas.md docs/product/roles.md

  2. 2

    Plan and design

    A vision, a minimum viable product and a now, next, later roadmap; how the products feed each other; a versioning rule; an honest check of what the organization can support.

    docs/product/roadmap.md docs/product/reference-design.md docs/product/maturity.md dbt/seeds/product_prioritization.csv

  3. 3

    Engineer and operationalize

    Pipelines, metadata and the governance policies: definitions, ethics, lifecycle, quality, security and privacy.

    dbt/models docs/playbook/onboard-a-source.md docs/product/governance-coverage.md docs/product/age-consent-rules.md

  4. 4

    Package and deliver

    One label per product: name, version, quality grade, type, owner, consumers, terms and support; a delivery channel, release management and a retirement plan.

    products/DP4/product.yml docs/product/terms/DP4.md products/CHANGELOG.md docs/product/decommission-example.md

  5. 5

    Monitor and observe

    Two separate views: inside (is the product healthy) and outside (is anyone using it, and is it moving the goal).

    scripts/build_product_health.py dbt/models/phase6/mart_goal_scorecard.sql docs/product/feedback-loop.md

Product, not project. A project ends at delivery. A product has an owner, users, versions, health measures and a retirement plan.

What this means. Before anything is built, we say who it is for and who owns it. After it ships, we watch whether it is healthy and whether anyone uses it.

What this means. Privacy is a stage of the work, not a step at the end: the rules are written before the pipelines and checked after.

What to do. Follow a product through the five stages using the files linked on each card.

What to do. Read the engineer stage first: it lists the privacy rules and their tests.

Be careful. A project ends at delivery. A product keeps an owner, users, versions, health measures and a retirement plan.

For analysts

The method is described in this project's own words in docs/product/README.md. Structure informed by a published data-product guidance framework (cited once there); none of its text or figures is reproduced.

7 · Kids' privacy

How are children protected, by age?

Minors are guardian-only. 9% of families with a child under thirteen agreed to club marketing.

How are children protected, by age?

What this means. A club can only reach a family whose guardian agreed, with a record that shows what they agreed to. Counting linked households does not put anyone on a contact list.

What this means. Every minor row is guardian-only, stricter than the laws we read. The youngest child in a household decides.

What to do. Ask guardians for club marketing consent at registration.

What to do. Verify the rules still marked unverified and add their sources.

Be careful. This is project policy, not legal advice. Whether a club is a third party is a question for counsel.

10 · Watching

How does watching a product change what we build?

Monitoring found a gap, a fix list targeted it, and the roadmap changed.

How does watching a product change what we build?

  1. Monitoring shows a gap (DP4 and DP1 health). The US league source grades C for both counting players and placing them on the map, with the same score, 82.1 (target X: at least 85). One check sets it: Arrived on time, which is the share of known US leagues that sent a file (82.1%). Every other check is A or B. At the market level, DP4 reports 14 of 76 known leagues sent no file, and 5 markets at grade C and 5 at D for placing players on the map. The weakest individual leagues are disproportionately the ones that send spreadsheets: 3 of 8 spreadsheet leagues are graded C for the map, against 1 of 40 on the recommended software.
  2. A cause is found (the fix list). For 14 US leagues the top fix is "send this season's registration file, in any format" (the second most common first fix for a US league, after "the form never mentioned marketing", which is the first fix for 44 leagues). The fix list gives each league its few most costly problems in plain words (share_ops__league_fix_list, Phase 5 dq_fix_list).
  3. The fix is applied at the source (the fix list, not a new standard). The data standard already asks for what the products need (the 3-digit ZIP is REQUIRED, ADR-018), so the standard does not change. What changes is that each league is sent its fix list.
  4. The next season shows whether it worked (a simulation, labelled). The Phase 5 fix-list demo (make fix-list-demo) simulates half of the struggling leagues acting on their fix list in the final season. In that simulated scenario the leagues that acted rose from 97.2 to 99.1 for counting and from 91.3 to 95.5 for placing players on the map, and from 59.5 to 63.2 for contacting families, while the struggling leagues that did not act went from 94.1 to 91.8 for the map. It is a scenario on a copy of the warehouse, not an observed result. It also narrows the girls' share bounds only slightly: the larger uncertainty is the leagues that send no file at all, which a fix list does not remove.
  5. The roadmap changes (proposal, not built). Because the largest remaining gap is leagues that send no file, DP1's next minor version would add a "no file received" alert to the league scorecard and a one-page request to the league (the fix-list letter on the Data quality page), and DP4's next version would show, beside each market's grade, how much of the grade is limited by leagues that sent nothing. Neither is built; both would be scored in product_prioritization.csv like any other item.
