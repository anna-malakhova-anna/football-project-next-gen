Identity: can the numbers be trusted?

Matching people across sources is the hard part. The generator's answer key grades it; real projects have no key.

What this shows. How pairwise precision and recall of the final probabilistic matcher move as the automatic-merge threshold changes, counting record pairs merged automatically (before any steward review), graded against the synthetic answer key. The dashed line is the chosen threshold; the shaded band is the probable range sent for review.

What it does not show. Performance on real data. The population is synthetic: phone numbers never change and one nickname list covers every nickname, so real data would score lower. The threshold was chosen once, before this chart.

Sibling and twin false merges by threshold: final matcher, automatic merges, counted as clustersSynthetic evaluation — not observable in production

What this shows. In plain terms: merging siblings is not just a counting error; it mixes two children's personal data, which is a privacy incident. The chart counts, at each threshold, the predicted people (clusters) that wrongly hold two children of one household, for the final matcher's automatic merges. The dashed line is the chosen threshold; the shaded band is the probable range sent for review.

Design choice. The final matcher has a household guard against sibling merges, and a data steward reviews the borderline "probable" pairs. The threshold was chosen once, before this chart.

What it does not show. Record pairs: one wrongly merged person counts once here, but once per pair of records in the error table below. Nor that a merge is harmless, or real-world performance: the sibling and twin merges that remain are the incidents that matter.
