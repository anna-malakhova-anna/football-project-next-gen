Participation

How many play, and how sure are we?

7,430 unique children played in 2025, up from 1,138 in 2021. Younger entrants stay much longer than teenagers, and boys and girls stay at the same rate. The original question, condensed: how many people play, with the band that identity resolution leaves, and who stays. Retention is Kaplan-Meier from the first season, computed in SQL: a player who turns 18, or whose follow-up ends with the data, is censored, not counted as gone.

Unique participants, with identity bounds

What this shows. Registered participants per year (each person once), and the band that identity resolution alone could move the number by.

What it does not show. Total flag football participation: only registered participants in onboarded sources, not informal play, attendance or missing sources. 2021 is the first data year.

Reading note. The identity band is too narrow to see on this scale: at its widest it is 0.5% of the count, so the line and the band overlap (the numbers are in the table below). The rise from 1,138 in 2021 to 7,430 in 2025 is mostly markets launching in successive years in this synthetic world, so it is not the same programme growing sixfold.

Participants by country, latest year

What this shows. Participants by country in the latest year, as two views of the same children side by side.

Design choice. "Attributed" counts each child once, in the country of their latest registration, so it adds up to a total. "Present" counts a child in every country they played in.

What it does not show. A global total: the "present" view must never be summed, since children who played in two countries would be counted twice. Small country counts are hidden rather than shown.

Girls' share of youth participation

What this shows. In plain terms: flag football is moving steadily towards gender parity.

What it does not show. Why the share is moving. It uses sex as recorded at registration, not gender identity. Children with no recorded sex stay in the denominator, so the share is slightly understated. The axis is zoomed in, which makes the rise look steeper than it is.

Retention by entry age band (Kaplan-Meier, with 95% intervals)

What this shows. In plain terms: children who start young are far more likely to keep playing.

Design choice. A player who turns 18, or whose records simply end, is treated as "no longer observed" (censored), not as having quit. Steps with fewer than 10 players at risk are not shown.

What it does not show. Why players leave, or what the curves would be for adults continuing after 17 (this synthetic world has no adult continuation).

Cohort triangle: share of each entry cohort still registered

What this shows. For each entry year, the share of that cohort registered again one, two and three years later (only years that have happened). Retention drops each year, to roughly a third of a cohort by year three.

What it does not show. Retention corrected for ageing out: older youth disappear because they turn 18, not because they chose to leave. The corrected version is in the table below.

Entrants aged 14 to 17: naive vs censored vs the truthSynthetic evaluation — not observable in production

What this shows. In plain terms: if you count a 17-year-old who turns 18 as a dropout, teenage retention looks 22 to 24 points worse than it really is. Treating the birthday as censoring lands near the true churn.

Design choice. This chart is the justification for the censoring rule. For younger entrants, who do not turn 18 within the five-year window, the naive and corrected curves are the same, so the rule only matters for teens.

What it does not show. Real churn: the truth here is the generator's own parameters, so this shows the rule works on known data, not what real players do.
